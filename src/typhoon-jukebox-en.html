About

How the sound is made

A typhoon's satellite imagery has always been silent. This jukebox gives it a voice -- turning a typhoon's life, from birth to dissipation, into a typhoon symphony: wind speed sets the tempo, pressure sets the bass, and every step up (or a landfall) rings out a metallic hit.

Sound mapping (symphonic version): wind speed sets the pitch, carried by lead strings whose long notes overlap and glide into each other with a reverb tail; a string pad cycles Am–F–C–G underneath for the whole piece; a timpani-and-low-string pulse gets denser and heavier as wind speed rises; French horns join at Category 3, full brass joins at Category 4; as the storm nears its peak the orchestration, volume and a timpani roll all climb together, with a brass chord slamming down at the very top, then the whole layer falls away as the storm weakens. The bass line instead tracks the current pressure -- the bigger the pressure drop, the lower and heavier the double bass, bassoon and tuba get; the faster the storm intensifies, the denser the notes. Wind is a string tremolo plus a breathy flute (continuous, no noise floor), rain is harp arpeggios and plucked-string raindrops, thunder is a timpani roll plus low brass; landfall percussion uses low timpani with a very quiet cymbal brush for every category -- and its loudness scales with the wind speed at the moment of landfall. Everything is synthesized in real time; no samples are used.

Data sources

Data source: Wikipedia, "2026 Pacific typhoon season" (JMA naming and peak-intensity records, JTWC category), checked as of 2026-11-11; the typhoon season is not yet over. Typhoon No. 32, Cempaka, has received its final update: it formed as a tropical depression on November 5, 2026, was named on November 7, reached its peak of 155 km/h (JMA 10-minute sustained wind, roughly 85 kt) and a minimum pressure of 955 hPa (JMA "Very Strong Typhoon") on November 10, weakened to a severe tropical storm (60 kt, 985 hPa, at 29.2°N, 151.0°E) at 09:00 UTC on November 11, and was declared extratropical by the JMA at 12:00 UTC that same day, ending its life as a tropical cyclone. This page is a static page that does not update automatically day to day; its data is current as of 2026-11-11, and any typhoons after that date are not included here.

Every other storm's peak wind speed, pressure and lifespan shown here are the real recorded values; the wind-speed curve used for playback is a sonified interpretation generated from that real peak and lifespan (including Bavi's eyewall-replacement dip and its two landfalls, anchored to its best-track positions), and the pressure shown during playback is an estimate derived from wind speed.

Maps and icons

Both maps use real Esri World Imagery satellite tiles as their base layer (loaded live over the network, so coastlines follow the real imagery), with the selection box, hard boundaries, spawn point and track drawn on top. Each storm also links from the player to NASA Worldview, where you can view the real satellite cloud imagery (VIIRS true-color) from the middle of its life. The icons are a new hand-drawn nine-piece set by Zhang.

This page turns typhoon data into sound as a demonstration piece -- it is not an official warning. For evacuation and other official guidance, always follow your local meteorological agency.
